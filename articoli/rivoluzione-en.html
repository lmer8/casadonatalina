I Want to Believe There Will Be a Revolution, but We'll Still Be the Same Old Unemployed People

I imagine you've all seen the images of the protests that have taken place in various Italian cities over the past few days: marches, clashes, looting… you've probably formed your own opinions. So I'm not going to recount the events; I want to share a reflection on them. And I'd like to start with these words by Michele Serra:

There is a devastating sense of social powerlessness which, combined with the monoculture of consumerism, leads these furious children (some as young as twelve) to loot sneaker shops, the most expensive pieces of plastic in history, mysteriously elevated to the status of symbols of prestige (do you know who ultimately won, the bourgeoisie or the proletariat? The shoes won). (…) There is nothing promising about looting a shop; there is only the pitiful subjection of the poor to the glitter of commodities.

My translation of Michele Serra, L'Amaca, 20 October 2026.

Michele Serra has always been a good writer, but I think he has outdone himself here with the incisiveness of this summary. The phrase "devastating social powerlessness" evokes people who are unable to change their circumstances, imagine a different future or find a recognised place in society. Yet the desire for recognition does not disappear. It can be channelled towards what the market offers: objects that promise identity, belonging and prestige. Sneakers thus become more than just shoes. They become a way of saying, "I exist too, I matter too, I can have what everyone else has."

Looting looks like an act of rupture: rules are broken, property is attacked, the social order is challenged. And yet—and this, to me, is the painful paradox—if the goal is to seize the very objects that embody commercial prestige, the rebellion does not challenge the dominant value system: it confirms its power. The point is not necessarily to challenge the existence of a hierarchy, but to challenge one's place at the bottom of it.

To be clear: wanting an expensive pair of sneakers is not in itself a form of alienation, and poverty does not automatically produce anger or violence. The problem arises when people's social worth is measured by what they own, while their real opportunities to gain recognition, autonomy and participation seem out of reach.

What I find particularly disturbing is that an economic system can even benefit from a rebellion that cannot imagine alternatives to the system itself. The shop is wrecked, but the desire driving the looting remains untouched. The owner of the shoes changes; their symbolic power does not.

I want to believe there will be a revolution, that my generation and the one after us will finally say (together): "Oh, come on, enough with taking the piss out of us! We studied, we worked, we did everything we were supposed to do… and we can't afford a home, let alone hope for a pension in the future. We're permanently precarious, if not completely unemployed, so you know what? Let's turn the whole thing upside down!!!"

Revolution is a fantasy of redemption. For a moment, faced with a society that seems unjust, you can imagine everything coming apart, contradictions exploding, someone finally saying enough is enough. Powerlessness turning into power, those pushed to the margins no longer being invisible.

But then comes the less epic thought: what do we, the people the system has already left behind, actually stand to gain? Even if everything blew up, we might find ourselves the next day exactly where we were before. Still precarious or unemployed, still trying to prove we have something left to offer, still having to adapt. And doing personal branding, telling ourselves that we simply need to learn to play by the rules a little better, because we are so convinced that this economic system is the best of all possible worlds.

Spoiler: it isn't.
