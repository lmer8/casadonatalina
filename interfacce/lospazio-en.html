Who Does Space Belong To?

October 2026

The Interface | Technology, Institutions, Society

Who does space belong to?

Everyone, no one, or whoever has a lot of money?

I read an excellent article by Antonio Ghezzi[1], which begins like this: “There is a pasture open to everyone. Every shepherd in the village brings their sheep there. At some point, everyone does the math: if I add one more sheep to my flock, all the profit is mine, while the cost (a little less grass) is shared among everyone. Why not? It pays off. It makes sense. One person does it, then a neighbour, then everyone. And the pasture dies. This is the ‘tragedy of the commons’, described by biologist Garrett Hardin in Science in 1968. Today, that pasture is a few hundred kilometres above our heads: low Earth orbit. In 1978, NASA scientist Donald Kessler described the orbital version of this tragedy and its consequences. Every additional satellite increases the risk of collisions. Every collision produces debris; every fragment can trigger further collisions. Beyond a certain density, the chain reaction becomes self-sustaining, and entire orbital bands become unusable for decades. This is known as Kessler syndrome. (…) The space economy is an enormous opportunity, including for Italy. But every common pasture needs rules to be written before the grass runs out, not afterwards.”

Reading Ghezzi’s article made me wonder, not for the first time, who space belongs to, and who it should belong to. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty[2] makes states responsible for the activities of their nationals, including companies, but there is no authority coordinating access to orbit. So who should make the decisions, and with what legitimacy? How do we develop technical agreements that then become rules (standards, guidelines on debris, coordination between agencies)? At the moment, the situation is rather confused, with old, vague and unenforced agreements, treaties that not everyone signs, and conflicting interests[3]. If you would like to explore the subject further, you will find some sources at the end of this article.

What seems fairly clear to me is that unrestricted access without regulation tends to lead to disaster. And here the question inevitably becomes political: how far can we allow private interests (legitimate interests, of course) — in other words, the economic interests of those seeking to make money by using a shared space — to prevail over the need to ensure that space remains available to everyone? I am not referring only to the moral right to enjoy something beautiful: a beach, a river, a mountain, a sea, a forest. These should belong to everyone in the sense that everyone should have the right to enjoy them. If someone buys these spaces and turns them into a source of personal profit, they deprive some people of an important opportunity. But there is also the damage done when the use or enjoyment of a space is taken away from everyone by spoiling it or making it unusable through commercial exploitation. And there is a third point, even more serious: if we accept that whoever has the financial means to exploit or control a part of a space, or access to it, can take control of it away from the community, we are also giving them permission to exercise a far more dangerous kind of control.

Imagine someone who controls certain satellite systems. Since orbital space is not infinite — in fact, it is rather crowded — anyone who needs to use those satellites will obviously have to pay this person for access. So, to connect to the internet where terrestrial networks cannot reach (at sea, in flight, in remote areas, in war zones), you will have to pay. And the person who controls the satellite system can always decide not to provide the service, right? After all, it is their company and their profit: we have granted them a licence to occupy part of an orbit and make money from it! And so we find ourselves in a situation where a country could be invaded militarily by a stronger power and might want to use drones to defend itself, yet depend on a commercial satellite connection to do so. It might then be unable to defend itself because the owner of the satellite company decides not to provide the service, simply because they like the other side better (which often translates into “the other side can pay more”). Now, I don't want to be melodramatic, but you do realise that when one country invades another, the result is not just a change of president, don't you? You do realise that war means deaths — often civilians, elderly people and children — and massacres? So, by deciding that someone must be allowed to make money from their space business, we have given them the right to decide that an invaded country cannot defend itself, and that an invading country can kill people in the other country, because it has the money to control the technologies it needs. But hey, it's the free market, baby.
